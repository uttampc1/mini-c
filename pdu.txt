Why you cannot run a cooktop on pure PoF:

1. The Efficiency Penalty: Photovoltaic Power Converters (PPCs) at the receiving end of a PoF link operate at roughly 50% efficiency at best. If you tried to deliver 5,000W to a cooktop, you would have to fire a 10,000W laser into the fiber.
2. Extreme Heat Generation: The 5,000W of "lost" energy inside the cooktop’s receiver wouldn't heat your food; it would instantly melt the photovoltaic converter and the fiber itself.
3. Fiber Material Constraints: Standard optical fibers have microscopic glass cores (9 to 62.5 microns). Forcing thousands of watts of laser energy into a core that small creates extreme power density, causing a catastrophic phenomenon known as the "fiber fuse"—where the glass literally boils and destroys the cable. Pushing higher wattages requires thick, heavy multi-mode or large-core specialty fibers (up to 800um), eliminating any space-saving benefits over copper.

• The PoF Solution: Optical leak-detection networks utilize PoF to run remote, water-activated optical switches or liquid-sensing micro-nodes. The lack of metal eliminates the risk of an electrical short circuit if the sensor itself gets submerged in a leak.

Data centers rely on massive Uninterruptible Power Supply (UPS) battery rooms and diesel backup generators. These systems operate with incredibly high Direct Current (DC) voltages and generate immense electromagnetic fields when they turn on.
• The Problem: The heavy electromagnetic interference (EMI) from a mega-generator kicking in can distort the data coming from battery health monitoring sensors.
• The PoF Solution: Fiber lines are entirely immune to EMI. PoF can deliver steady, noise-free power to the battery-cell monitoring chips in a massive UPS room, ensuring accurate state-of-charge data even during a catastrophic power failure.

To evaluate Power-over-Fiber (PoF) for your research, you must analyze its end-to-end efficiency chain. PoF does not save energy in terms of "wall plug" efficiency; rather, it trades raw power efficiency for massive physical, safety, and infrastructure footprint savings at the edge.

1. How Much Power It Takes to Drive the Laser

To deliver a small amount of usable electrical power to your isolated smart PDU node, you must deal with compounding efficiency losses at three stages:
1. The Laser Diode Driver: Converting wall-plug AC/DC power into laser light (Wall-Plug Efficiency).
2. The Fiber Medium: Minimal attenuation losses over short smart-home distances, but minor coupling losses exist.
3. The Photovoltaic Power Converter (PPC): Transforming the laser light back into usable electricity at the edge node.

The Power Calculation Example

High-efficiency direct-diode lasers operate at roughly 50% to 60% electrical-to-optical efficiency. Meanwhile, edge-node PPC converters operate at roughly 40% to 50% optical-to-electrical efficiency.
If your isolated edge sensor logic requires 5 Watts of usable DC electricity to run its microcontrollers and Wi-Fi chip:
• At the PPC (Edge): To get 5W out, you must shoot roughly 10 Watts of optical laser light into it (assuming a generous 50% PPC efficiency).
• At the Laser (Base): To generate 10W of laser light, the laser diode must pull roughly 17 to 20 Watts of electrical power from the wall plug (assuming 50-60% laser efficiency).
The Efficiency Verdict: To power a 5W edge device, the system consumes roughly 20W total. It operates at an end-to-end efficiency of about 25%.

2. What Do You "Save" at the Edge?

While PoF suffers an efficiency penalty, it yields massive savings in terms of operational footprint, safety costs, and hardware lifespan at the edge node:

📉 Power Savings at the Edge (Indirect Energy Savings)

• Zero Active Cooling for Sensors: In environments like smart panels or data center racks, copper-wired monitoring systems can generate parasitic heat from resistive copper loops. PoF components generate almost no heat outside of the PPC conversion point, keeping edge nodes cooler.
• Elimination of local AC/DC Power Supplies: You completely remove the need to place bulky transformer bricks, voltage regulators, or local batteries inside the high-power panel. This reduces the baseline "vampire draw" (idle power consumption) of multiple isolated power bricks.

🛠️ Massive Infrastructure & Bill of Materials (BOM) Savings

• Heavy Shielding Costs: To run copper data and power lines near an induction cooktop, high-power microwave magnetron, or data center busbar, you must buy expensive, heavily shielded copper cables (like Category 7A/8 or armored cables) to block electromagnetic interference (EMI). Glass fiber requires zero shielding, radically dropping cable thickness, weight, and material costs.
• Surge Protection Devices (SPDs): Traditional smart panels require complex surge-arresting circuits to protect low-voltage control chips from high-voltage spikes. Because glass fiber acts as a 100% galvanic isolation barrier, you can completely eliminate those expensive safety components from the board design.
• Maintenance & Lifespan Savings: High currents and harsh kitchen environments cause copper contacts to corrode, oxidize, and loosen over time due to thermal expansion (heating up and cooling down). Glass fiber does not corrode, meaning edge nodes have a vastly longer operational lifespan with zero terminal tightening required.

When writing your proposal, frame PoF not as an energy-saving technology, but as an operational insurance policy. You spend an extra 15 watts of baseline power at the main hub to buy absolute immunity to lightning/surges, total elimination of EMI signal corruption, zero fire/spark risks, and a radically simplified edge hardware layout.

To properly engineer your Power-over-Fiber (PoF) architecture, your research will need to define a strict optical link power budget. This ensures the laser provides enough energy to account for all system losses, leaving exactly what the edge node requires to operate.
Here are the mathematical formulas and engineering frameworks for your research paper.

Part 1: Mathematical Formulas for PoF Power Budgets

The goal of a PoF power budget is to calculate the required Electrical Input Power (\(P_{elec\_in}\)) at the base unit to guarantee a required Electrical Output Power (\(P_{elec\_out}\)) at the isolated edge node.

1. The Core Power Budget Equation

The end-to-end efficiency chain is multiplicative:
\(P_{elec\_out}=P_{elec\_in}\cdot \eta _{laser}\cdot \eta _{coupling}\cdot A_{fiber}\cdot \eta _{PPC}\)
Where:
• \(P_{elec\_out}\) = Usable electrical power at the edge node (Watts)
• \(P_{elec\_in}\) = Wall-plug electrical power consumed by the transmitter (Watts)
• \(\eta _{laser}\) = Wall-plug efficiency of the laser diode (typically \(0.40\) to \(0.60\))
• \(\eta _{coupling}\) = Optical coupling efficiency into the fiber (typically \(0.85\) to \(0.95\))
• \(A_{fiber}\) = Total attenuation/transmission factor of the fiber medium
• \(\eta _{PPC}\) = Efficiency of the Photovoltaic Power Converter at the edge (typically \(0.35\) to \(0.50\))

2. Calculating Fiber Attenuation (\(A_{fiber}\))

Fiber loss is logarithmic and calculated using the attenuation coefficient (\(\alpha \)), measured in decibels per kilometer (\(\text{dB/km}\)), over the fiber length (\(L\) in km).
\(\text{Loss}_{dB}=\alpha \cdot L+\text{Loss}_{connectors}\)
Once you have the total loss in decibels, convert it to a linear transmission factor (\(A_{fiber}\)):
\(A_{fiber}=10^{-\left(\frac{\text{Loss}_{dB}}{10}\right)}\)
• Note for your research: For home electronics (\(L < 0.05 \text{ km}\)) and data centers (\(L < 0.3 \text{ km}\)), fiber attenuation loss is almost completely negligible (often \(< 0.1 \text{ dB}\)). Your primary losses will be the laser efficiency (\(\eta _{laser}\)) and PPC efficiency (\(\eta _{PPC}\)).

Part 2: Simultaneous Power and Data via WDM

Using multiple fibers for power and data is bulky. Wavelength Division Multiplexing (WDM) solves this by sending different "colors" of light down a single strand of glass simultaneously. Because photons of different wavelengths do not collide or interact, power and data can share the same channel without interfering.
To achieve this in your architecture, you use a WDM Coupler/Filter at both ends.

The Dual-Wavelength Architecture Design:

• The Power Lane (Downstream): A high-power laser operates at \(975 \text{ nm}\) or \(1480 \text{ nm}\). These specific wavelengths are chosen because they match the peak absorption efficiency of Gallium Arsenide (GaAs) or Indium Phosphide (InP) Photovoltaic Power Converters.
• The Data Lane (Upstream/Downstream): Standard data transceivers modulate low-power communication pulses at \(1310 \text{ nm}\) or \(1550 \text{ nm}\).
